We have just had another Court House meeting
—and such a mecting! Why are the papers of
the city, those. faithful heralds of all kinds of in-
telligence, silent? "What will the South think,
when it is told, that on Tuesday-evening, the 7th
of May, a large concourse of citizens assembled
‘at the Court House, to: listen to an anti-slavery
speech from the lips of that notorious abolitionist,
the Hon, Thomas Morris? Will not the Lexing-
ton and Cincinnati Turnpike project be abandon-
ed? and the Charleston and Ohio Rail Road gen-
try—how will they endure it? Just when the
South is about opening its arms to embrace Cin-
cinnati, to see Abolitionism rearing its snaky
crest even in the Court House of the city—O, it
is too bad!
Jan, 22d, 1836, 500 or 600 of the citizens of
Cincinnati met in the Court House, for the pur-
pose of passing sentence of death on abolition.
They resolved every thing for slavery, and no-
thing for freedom. hey resolved that they would
lawfully suppress the publication of the Philan-
thropist, kéep out of their houses, counting-rooms,
and work-shops, all similar inflammatory publica-
tions, and, if nothing else would do, join in re-
commending to the legislature to pass laws against
the agitation of the slavery question.
March 9th, 1839, another Court House meet-
ing was called, of equivocal respectability, which
passed divers flaming ‘resolutions, assuring the
South! of the soundness of Cincinnati, her hatred
of Abolitionists, and her love for Colonizationists,
‘This meeting was one of the results of Mr; Gur-
ley’s efforts in this place, and great reports con-
cerning it have been published in the Christion
Statesman and Colonization Herald. Then comes
our turn.
