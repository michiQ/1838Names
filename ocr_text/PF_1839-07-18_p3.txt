The abolition excitement, when at its
height, set many pens in motion, and in ad-
dition to the fierce newspaper discussions
which were provoked,-a lumber of volumes
of graver pretensions on both sides of the
question were presented to the public. These,
however, as far as our experience went, and
we took oceasion to look into’ 2 majority: of
them, were litle better, as arguments, than
the angry squibbings of the periodical press,
and threw as'little light upon the important
subject under consideration, being for the
Tost part the effusions of passion and pre-
judice, sometimes a mere commodity made
for sale and to suit a sectional market, and
not unfrequently they were neither more nor
leas than popularity traps, to gain personal
influence. ‘To toil through the tedious pages
of such productions as these was labor thrown’
away, and we had resolved to read no more
upon the subject, leaving the champions, pro
and con, to helabor each other as “temper
might dictate. To this resolution we adhered
until the work above mentioned was placed
in our hands by a friend, with an earnest re-
quest to look into it with care, as he believed
that it containéd much toarrest attention, and
a compliance with his desire enables us to
say, that a volume has at last appeared upon
the absorbing question of. American slavery
which really deserves a caréful and calm epu-
sideration from the friends of colonization, of.
abolition, and of negro servitudé as it exists.
The writer, who dates from the District of
Columbia, professes to be a Northern man
of considerable Southern experience, and in
his present principles,is an advocate of colo-
nization, if necessary, through the aid of the
general government, but he has the rare me-
Tit of entering into the feelings of men of all
sides, of anderstanding their motives, of ap-
Preciating their merits, and also of seeing
clearly, the faults into which they have allow-
ed themselves to be hurried by the fury of
the moment. He assumes, and we think,
when the failure. of others is remembered,
admirably maintains thé attitude of a cool,
dispassionate observer, He succeeds so well
in this that his home truths will probably not

either party. They who hold with McDuf-
fie, that the relation of master and slave is
the most perfect form of civil society; will
like the observations of an American as little

monise the North and the South than any

each otfer.

The writer acknowledges all the evils of
slavery as fully as the most ardent. abolition-
ist could desire—he grants its baneful effects

prosperity of the country in which it exists;
but the South finds in him an able supporter

cism; and while pleading the cause and ex-
plaining the circumstances of the South with
what may be regarded as enlightened South-
ern zeal, he does full justice to the motives
of those at the North fo whose agency recent
excitements are chiefly owing, at the same
time demonstrating the mischiefs caused by
an interference which has rolled back the
current of anti-slavery feeling in the slave-
holding-states, But he is far from yielding
assent to the monstrous doctrines put forth a
year or two since, that censorship is to con-
trol the press, terrorism to interfere with the

silence free discussion upon any subject—even
upon that of slavéry. In this view, while
