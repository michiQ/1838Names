Streetcar Discrimination in Philadelphia

Black Philadelphians were fighting for the right to ride long before the streetcar. In 1837 the Colored American denounced the treatment of a Black man turned away from a New York omnibus, and in November 1840 Westward Keeling of Philadelphia bought space in the Public Ledger to protest that his 14-year-old daughter Araminta had been made to ride on top of a stagecoach in the cold. When the city's horse-drawn passenger railways began running in 1858, they refused to carry Black riders inside, or made them stand on the front platform. This timeline follows the ejections and attacks, and the organized answer to them: letters, mass meetings, petitions, lectures and court cases, through the state law of March 1867 that forbade the exclusion, and what happened after it.
